How to take Ayurvedic medicines

Forms of medicine

Churna
Fine powder of dried herbs. Mixed with the vehicle (water, honey, ghee) and swallowed.
Kwatha / Kashaya
Decoction: coarse herbs boiled in water and reduced. Best fresh, warm and on an empty stomach; ready-made liquids and tablets also exist.
Vati / Gutika
Tablet or pill.
Guggulu
Tablets made with guggul resin (Commiphora), mostly used for joints, fat and skin.
Ghrita
Herbs cooked into ghee. Fat-soluble herbs reach the tissues and the brain; often taken early morning.
Taila
Medicated oil, for massage, nasal drops (nasya), enemas (basti) or occasionally by mouth.
Avaleha / Leha
Herbal jam made with sugar or jaggery. Not ideal in diabetes.
Asava / Arishta
Fermented liquid with naturally formed alcohol (usually 5–10%). Taken after meals with equal water.
Bhasma / Pishti
Ash or fine powder of processed minerals, metals or shells.
Rasa / Rasaushadhi
Herbo-mineral medicines, usually with processed mercury and sulphur. Supervision only.

Timing and vehicle

Empty stomach
Early morning, before food, for strongest action (decoctions, ghee, purgatives).
Before meals
About 15–30 minutes before food: digestive and lower-body disorders.
After meals
Within 30 minutes after food: upper-body, chest and heart conditions; easier on the stomach.
Bedtime
Laxatives, sleep medicines, Triphala.
Anupana
The vehicle taken with or after a medicine (warm water, honey, ghee, milk, buttermilk). It helps carry the medicine and is part of the prescription.
Honey
Never heated or mixed into hot liquids (the classics warn against heated honey); never for babies under 1 year.
2× / 3× daily
The dose shown is per time unless the table says "a day".

General rules

  • Follow the diet (pathya) during treatment; the classics say medicine without diet is of little use.
  • Keep at least 30 minutes between Ayurvedic medicines and modern tablets, and tell both doctors.
  • Stop and see a doctor if you get a rash, jaundice, dark urine, severe stomach upset or palpitations.
  • Store powders airtight and dry; discard ones that clump, smell or change colour.

The three doshas, in brief

Vata (air and space) governs movement, nerves and breathing; excess causes dryness, pain and anxiety. Pitta (fire and water) governs digestion, heat and metabolism; excess causes burning, acidity and inflammation. Kapha (earth and water) governs structure, lubrication and immunity; excess causes heaviness, mucus and weight gain. Classical treatment balances the dosha involved, which is why a vaidya may choose different medicines for the same disease.

Trusted Ayurveda centres

Long-established hospitals run by the government, charitable trusts or hereditary physician families, with qualified doctors, their own or licensed medicines, and inpatient and outpatient care. Listed from oldest to newest.

How to recognise a genuine centre

  • Doctors hold a BAMS or MD (Ayurveda) degree and are registered with a state board or NCISM.
  • NABH accreditation (India's hospital quality standard) is a strong sign of safety and standards.
  • A proper consultation before any therapy, with your blood tests and current medicines reviewed.
  • Be wary of resorts or spas that promise cures, guaranteed results, or tell you to stop modern medicines.
  • Government institutes (NIA, ITRA, AIIA, CCRAS, government colleges) are the lowest-cost; private centres usually quote package prices per week. Ask in advance.

Details are from each institution's own and official sources as of November 2026; bed numbers and services change, so confirm directly before travelling. Listing is not an endorsement of any individual doctor or treatment.

Editorial standards

This site aims to be a source you can trust: honest about what Ayurveda says, honest about what science shows, and clear about safety. These are the rules every page follows.

How content is made

  • Primary sources only. Classical texts are cited by book and chapter; doses come only from Government of India guidelines, never invented. Each page names its dose source.
  • Evidence graded separately. What tradition says and what trials show are kept apart. Ratings are based on published human studies, not testimonials.
  • Modern medicine alongside. Every condition states what conventional care recommends, and where it is clearly better.
  • Safety first. Warning signs appear at the top of every page; toxic and metal-containing medicines are always flagged.
  • No cure claims. We do not say any treatment cures a disease unless strong evidence shows it.
  • AI-assisted, human-verified. Pages were compiled with AI assistance from the sources named. Each is marked with its review status until checked by qualified reviewers.

Independence and corrections

  • No paid placement. No company or hospital can pay to be listed, ranked or recommended. Any future commercial links will be labelled and kept off the medical content.
  • No sponsored medical content and no advertising on condition pages.
  • Review cycle. Every page shows when it was last updated and is re-checked at least once a year, or when new guidelines are issued.
  • Corrections. Errors are fixed promptly and noted in the change log below.
  • Privacy. The routine planner stores your details only in your own browser. Nothing is sold or shared.

Everyday ailments: home remedies

Simple kitchen remedies for minor problems, as published by the government's Central Council for Research in Ayurvedic Sciences (CCRAS) in Ayurvedic Home Remedies and the Ministry of Ayush self-care advisory. Basic first aid (rehydration, cooling burns) follows standard medical guidance.

  • Doses are for adults. 1 teaspoon = about 5 g or 5 ml.
  • If there is no relief in 2–3 days, see a doctor. Stop any remedy that causes discomfort.
  • Do not give home remedies to babies under 1 year without a doctor's advice; never give honey to babies.
  • Home remedies are an add-on, not a replacement for prescribed treatment. Tell your doctor what you take.

The classical pillars of prevention

  • Daily routine (Dinacharya): waking early, cleansing, oil massage, exercise to half strength, regular meals and sleep. Plan yours →
  • Seasonal routine (Ritucharya): adjusting food and activity to each season (Ashtanga Hridaya, Sutra 3).
  • Foods to eat daily (Charaka Samhita, Sutra 5): aged rice, green gram, rock salt, amla, barley, milk, ghee and honey; avoid regular heavy, dried or stale foods.
  • Never suppress natural urges (Charaka Samhita, Sutra 7), and practise good conduct and a calm mind (Sadvritta).
  • The three pillars of life: food, sleep and a disciplined life (Charaka Samhita, Sutra 11).

Ayurvedic prevention does not replace

  • Vaccinations for children and adults
  • Regular checks of blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol, and age-appropriate cancer screening
  • Dental check-ups and eye tests

Traditional Healers & Nature Cure

India's living healing traditions beyond classical Ayurveda: tribal healers honoured by the Government of India, and long-established nature-cure (naturopathy) and Tibetan medicine (Sowa-Rigpa) institutions.

Please read first

  • Folk and tribal remedies have not been tested in clinical studies. Claims of curing cancer, kidney disease or diabetes are the healers' own and are unverified.
  • For cancer, kidney failure, epilepsy, diabetes or any serious illness, continue your regular treatment and tell your doctors about any herbal medicine you take.
  • This site does not publish these healers' formulas. Their knowledge belongs to their communities and is shared only with their consent.
  • Listing honours their work; it is not a medical endorsement. Confirm availability and fees directly before travelling.

Mineral medicines (Rasa Shastra): how they are made and how to stay safe

Rasa Shastra is the branch of Ayurveda that makes medicines from metals and minerals: mercury (Parada), sulphur (Gandhaka), iron (Lauha), gold (Svarna), mica (Abhraka), coral, pearl and others. These are the medicines marked Rasa · mineral on this site. The classical texts are the Rasa Ratna Samuccaya, Rasendra Chudamani, Rasarnava, Rasa Tarangini and Ayurveda Prakasha.

How a bhasma is made

  1. Shodhana (purification): the raw metal or mineral is heated, quenched and soaked in herbal juices, milk or oils to remove impurities.
  2. Bhavana (levigation): it is ground for hours with herbal juices or decoctions.
  3. Marana (incineration): pellets are sealed in clay pots and heated in measured fires (puta), often dozens of times.
  4. Amritikarana: a final step to remove remaining harmful qualities.

The classical quality tests (Bhasma Pariksha)

TestWhat it checks
RekhapurnataThe powder is fine enough to enter the lines of the fingertips.
VaritaraA pinch floats on still water (very fine and light).
NishchandratvaNo metallic shine remains.
ApunarbhavaThe metal cannot be recovered when the ash is reheated with reviving agents.
NirutthaNo metal reappears when heated with silver.
Dantagre kach-kach abhavaNo gritty feeling between the teeth.
NiramlaNo sour taste (for some bhasmas).

These tests check fineness and transformation. They cannot measure how much lead, mercury or arsenic a product contains: only laboratory testing can.

What the research shows

  • A 2004 study in JAMA found that 1 in 5 Ayurvedic products made in South Asia and sold in Boston stores contained potentially harmful levels of lead, mercury or arsenic.
  • A 2008 JAMA study of 193 medicines bought online found about 1 in 5 contained detectable lead, mercury or arsenic, and Rasa Shastra medicines were much more likely to contain metals than herbal-only ones (about 41% compared with 17%).
  • Ayurveda experts point out that properly prepared and correctly given Rasa medicines are considered safe in the tradition; the problem is that quality varies widely and a buyer cannot see it.

How to protect yourself

  • Take Rasa · mineral medicines only when prescribed by a qualified Ayurveda doctor (BAMS/MD), for the shortest time, at the prescribed dose.
  • Buy only from licensed, GMP-certified manufacturers; the label should carry a drug licence number. Ask for a heavy-metal test certificate.
  • Never give them to children, pregnant or breastfeeding women unless a specialist decides it is essential.
  • For use longer than a few weeks, ask for a blood lead test and kidney and liver tests.
  • Warning signs of metal poisoning: abdominal pain, constipation, tiredness, anaemia, tingling or weakness in hands and feet, memory or mood changes. Stop the medicine and see a doctor.
  • Herbal-only medicines (Herbal) are the first choice for most conditions on this site.

Sources: Saper RB et al., JAMA 2004;292:2868–73 and JAMA 2008;300:915–23; Ayurvedic Formulary of India; published bhasma standardisation studies (Ancient Science of Life, AYU).

Origins and philosophy of Ayurveda

Ayurveda (आयुर्वेद, "the knowledge of life") grew out of the Vedic world. Its ideas about the body, mind and medicine rest on Vedic hymns, the Upanishads, the Samkhya philosophy and yoga. This page traces those roots and links each to its text in the classical library.

1. The Vedas: the first healing hymns

  • Rigveda (c. 1500–1200 BCE): the Oshadhi Sukta (Rigveda 10.97) praises healing plants; the twin Ashvins are the physicians of the gods.
  • Atharvaveda (c. 1200–1000 BCE): the earliest collection of healing hymns, naming fevers, coughs, worms, wounds and the plants used against them. Sushruta calls Ayurveda an upanga (limb) of the Atharvaveda, and Charaka says a physician should be devoted to it.

2. The lineage of teaching

Both great Samhitas open with how knowledge came down to humans:

Charaka (Sutra 1)
Brahma → Daksha Prajapati → the Ashvins → Indra → Bharadvaja → Atreya Punarvasu → six pupils: Agnivesha, Bhela, Jatukarna, Parashara, Harita, Ksharapani. Agnivesha's text, revised by Charaka and completed by Dridhabala, is the Charaka Samhita.
Sushruta (Sutra 1)
Brahma → Prajapati → the Ashvins → Indra → Dhanvantari (as Divodasa, king of Kashi) → Sushruta and fellow pupils. This surgical school produced the Sushruta Samhita.

3. Philosophy: what the body and mind are made of

  • Samkhya: reality unfolds from Purusha (consciousness) and Prakriti (nature) into 25 principles: intellect, ego, mind, the senses and the five great elements. Charaka (Sharira 1) adopts this scheme to explain the person.
  • Pancha Mahabhuta: space, air, fire, water and earth make up everything, including foods and medicines. Their combinations give the three doshas: Vata (air and space), Pitta (fire and water), Kapha (water and earth).
  • Taittiriya Upanishad: the five sheaths (Pancha Kosha) of food, breath, mind, intellect and bliss, a model still used to describe whole-person care.
  • Prashna Upanishad: Prana and its divisions, the basis of the five Vayus (Prana, Udana, Samana, Vyana, Apana) through which Vata works.
  • Three pillars of life: food (ahara), sleep (nidra) and regulated conduct (brahmacharya), with the mind, soul and body as a tripod (Charaka Sutra 1 and 11).

4. Yoga: the sister science

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali set out the eight limbs, from ethics to meditation. Charaka describes yoga and liberation (moksha) in Sharira Sthana 1. Later the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and Gheranda Samhita added postures, breathing and cleansing practices, now suggested on each condition page under 🧘 Yoga & breathing.

Safety first

Taking a mineral (Rasa) medicine? Read how they are made and how to stay safe.

Before taking any medicine

  • See a registered Ayurveda doctor. Classical treatment depends on your prakriti (constitution), the dosha involved, digestion strength and season, which no website can judge.
  • Tell both your Ayurveda doctor and your regular doctor everything you take. Herbs interact with blood thinners, diabetes, blood-pressure, thyroid and psychiatric medicines.
  • All doses here are adult doses. Children, pregnant or breastfeeding women, the elderly and people with liver or kidney disease need individual dosing.
  • Buy from licensed manufacturers (GMP-certified, AYUSH licence number on the label).

What the tags mean

Herbal Plant-based. Usually lower risk, but can still cause side effects and interactions.

Rasa · mineral Herbo-mineral (rasaushadhi) medicines with processed mercury, sulphur, metals or minerals. This is the later Rasa Shastra tradition (c. 8th–16th century CE), not the oldest Samhitas. US studies have found lead, mercury or arsenic in about 1 in 5 Ayurvedic products sold online. Use only under supervision, for limited periods, from licensed makers; never for children or in pregnancy unless a specialist directs.

Toxic herb Contains a poisonous plant (e.g. Vishatinduka/nux vomica, Bhallataka, Vatsanabha/aconite) that is safe only after classical purification and in tiny doses. Never self-medicate.

Evidence ratings

Some evidence At least a few controlled trials show benefit for some medicines. Limited Mostly small or uncontrolled studies. Very limited Case reports or tradition only. A low rating means "not yet proven", not "proven useless".

About homeopathy

This site does not list homeopathic remedies. Australia's National Health and Medical Research Council reviewed the evidence in 2015 and found no health condition for which homeopathy works reliably better than placebo. Where a modern treatment clearly works better, each condition page says so under What modern medicine recommends.

Sources & method

The classical canon

  • Charaka Samhita: medicine; the core text, redacted c. 2nd century BCE–2nd century CE, later revised by Dridhabala.
  • Sushruta Samhita: surgery, anatomy, ENT, eye (c. early centuries CE).
  • Ashtanga Hridaya by Vagbhata (c. 7th century CE): a concise synthesis of both.
  • Madhava Nidana (c. 7th–8th century CE): diagnosis. It first describes conditions such as Amavata.
  • Kashyapa Samhita: paediatrics (Kaumarabhritya); first detailed account of Amlapitta.

The first three together are the Brihat Trayi ("great three"). Each condition page cites the chapters that describe it, abbreviated as Ch. (Charaka), Su. (Sushruta), AH (Ashtanga Hridaya), Su = Sutrasthana, Ni = Nidana, Chi = Chikitsa, Ut = Uttara, Sha = Sharira.

Doses and diet

The classics give doses in old units and assume a physician is adapting them. Medicine, dose, timing, vehicle (anupana), duration, diet (pathya/apathya) and referral criteria here come from the Ayurvedic Standard Treatment Guidelines (Ministry of AYUSH, Government of India, 2017). These were written by professors of national Ayurveda institutes from the same classics. Newer conditions use the Ministry of Health's Standard Treatment Guidelines in Ayurveda on Metabolic Disorders and on Musculoskeletal Disorders (DGHS, 2024 onwards), and the government NPCDCS-Ayurveda integration programme for high blood pressure. Each condition page names its source. "Level 1" is what a solo clinic gives; "Level 2" adds stronger or herbo-mineral medicines in a hospital setting (shown in Practitioner view).

Side effects and evidence ratings come from published clinical research and safety data, not from the classics.

Being added next

All 38 chapters of the Standard Treatment Guidelines are now included. Next come the remaining disease chapters of Charaka's treatment section (Chikitsasthana), with doses from the Ayurvedic Formulary of India: Raktapitta (bleeding disorders), Gulma (abdominal lumps), Rajayakshma (wasting disease), Unmada (psychosis), Shvayathu (swelling), Grahani (irritable bowel), Chhardi (vomiting), Visarpa (shingles and spreading skin infections), Madatyaya (alcohol disorders), Vrana (wounds), Hikka (hiccups) and Mutrakrichchhra (urinary infections). Constipation, indigestion, loss of appetite, IBS, hives, eczema and vitiligo have been added using medicine doses from the 2017 guidelines; they will be updated from the Ministry's 2026 skin-disorder guidelines once available. Still to come: fungal skin infection, hair loss, PCOS, infertility and sinusitis.
